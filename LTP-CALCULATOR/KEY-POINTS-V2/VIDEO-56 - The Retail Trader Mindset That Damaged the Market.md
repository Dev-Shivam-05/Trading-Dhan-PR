# The Retail Trader Mindset That Damaged the Market

## In one line

A thought experiment — what would happen if every retail trader in India became profitable at once — used to argue that universal profitability is arithmetically impossible in intraday derivatives and would wreck the market if it briefly occurred.

## When trading is actually a zero-sum game

- Pure intraday trading, where money goes in and comes out the same day, is zero-sum: one side's gain is the other side's loss.
- That is strictly true for intraday futures and options. The speaker deliberately excludes positional trades, BTST, writing strategies and long-term investing, where several participants can profit at the same time because the underlying assets themselves grow.
- Even in the zero-sum case both sides pay brokerage, so the broker, the exchange and the clearing corporation collect on every trade regardless of who wins.
- The counterparty is the person on the other side of your contract — the seller when you buy. Neither side knows the other; the clearing department manages the match.

## What SEBI's numbers show

- For the financial years 2022 to 2024, SEBI's study found 93% of retail traders in futures and options lost money; only about 7% made a profit.
- Within that profitable minority, only the top 1% made more than Rs 1 lakh. The rest of the gains were small.
- Retail traders lost roughly Rs 1.8 lakh crore over those three years, and that money went to proprietary trading firms, foreign institutions and domestic institutions.

## The hypothetical, step by step

- If all 1.5 crore retail traders profited, the money would have to come out of FIIs, DIIs, mutual funds and prop desks. Those players would not sit still — they would either pull capital out of India or build new strategies to win it back.
- A phase of guaranteed profits would create euphoria: people with no market knowledge entering, loans taken to trade, fixed deposits moved into stocks, valuations inflating far beyond fundamentals.
- With everyone holding and nobody selling, there would be no exit. The first wave of profit-booking would find no buyers, and the bubble would break into a sharp crash.
- Foreign money attracted by the boom would leave just as quickly. Liquidity would dry up completely, leaving no buyers and no sellers.
- Brokers and the exchange would first enjoy record volumes, STT and GST collections, then see that income disappear when the market emptied out.
- The speaker extends this to the wider economy: if trading were a guaranteed income, people would leave jobs, factories, farming and services, and GDP would suffer. When the bubble broke, those same people would have their savings gone and debt in their place.
- Regulators would be forced to intervene, tightening rules or restricting entry to restore stability.

## Why the speaker says it can never hold

- Markets need disagreement to function. If every participant held the same view, there would be no counterparty and trading would stop in a day.
- His comparison: not everyone can top an exam, and 1 lakh candidates cannot all score the same marks for 1,000 vacancies.
- The profitable minority are not people who win every day. He describes a typical profitable trader as someone who may trade only 200 days out of 365, lose on roughly half of them, and finish the year net positive.
- Those traders are disciplined, do not trade every single day, do not take ten or more call-put entries in a session, and treat a stop loss as a normal cost of the game.
- Retail is competing against institutions that spend heavily on research, tools, algorithmic trading and fund managers.

## What the video tells you to do

- Treat any claim that a system can make all traders profitable as a sales pitch, not a possibility.
- Accept losing trades as part of the method rather than as evidence the method failed.
- Trade selectively instead of daily, and keep entry count low.
- If you want a situation where broad profitability is genuinely possible, look at long-term investing in a growing market — index funds, ETFs, mutual funds — not intraday derivatives.

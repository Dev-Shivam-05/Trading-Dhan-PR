# Implied Volatility, Intrinsic Value and Time Value

## In one line

A live-market conversation showing why, when implied volatility collapses in a consolidating market, an option buyer can be completely right about direction and still end the trade with nothing.

## The setup being discussed

- Two traders — the speaker and Amit Bhakta, a trainer from Nagpur — sit in front of a live Nifty option chain with the candles and most Greeks switched off, so only the raw chain is visible.
- The market is consolidating around the 25,150–25,200 area and implied volatility has fallen sharply. Support is holding at the bottom and resistance has shifted lower.
- Time value across the near strikes has been almost completely drained. That is the whole subject of the discussion.

## Why the option buyer gets nothing

- At the money, the premium is about ₹55. A 40-point rise in the index should, on delta alone, add roughly ₹20 to that option.
- But the move takes time, and the time value bleeds out while the move happens. By the time the index reaches the target, the premium has not gained what the delta implied.
- The round trip is worse. The index goes from roughly 25,160 up to 25,200 and back to 25,170, and the same option that was ₹55 is now ₹50, then ₹48, then ₹45, then ₹44. Same index level, smaller premium.
- The net result is a trade worth 7 to 10 points. Brokerage takes that. The speakers repeat that the only certain outcome here is the brokerage bill.
- The people who made money in this session were the option writers, not the buyers.

## The deep-in-the-money workaround, and its limit

- The idea raised is to buy a deep in-the-money option whose time value has already gone to zero — in this session, the 25,300 put — and trade it like a futures contract, since it should then move roughly point for point with the spot.
- In practice it did not. The 25,300 put was ₹118–120 and after the whole move was ₹122. About ₹2 for a 25 to 30 point index swing.
- The reason given: a small amount of time value gets added back into the option as the move happens, and then taken out again on the way back. So the "zero time value" assumption does not survive a real move.
- The same loss pattern appeared at the 25,200 put. Both deep-in-the-money strikes ended in loss even though the index had returned to the buyer's entry level.

## What the reversal prices were saying

- On the call side, the reversal price of each strike sat almost on the strike itself — 25,200 showed about 25,205, 25,250 showed 25,250, 25,300 showed 25,300. That is, essentially no time value and nothing attractive on the call side.
- On the put side the reversal prices sat about 30 points below their strikes — the 25,200 put reversal showed 25,170, the 25,150 showed 25,125, the 25,100 showed 25,075.
- The reading of this: the chain was pricing roughly 30 points of downside potential and zero upside potential.
- At expiry these reversal prices converge to intrinsic value, so in a continued consolidation the put reversals would drift up to 25,180, 25,185, 25,190, 25,195 and the move would be over.

## What the video tells you to do

- Read time value on the chain before taking an options trade, not after.
- In a low implied-volatility consolidation, do not buy options. The speakers' verdict: if the futures move is 25 to 30 points and the option gives you 2, take the trade only if the futures leg makes sense, otherwise stay out.
- Set a level you will act on, and do not trade until the market reaches it. If it never reaches it, take no trade at all.
- If a position must be held here, the writing side is where the money went in this session.

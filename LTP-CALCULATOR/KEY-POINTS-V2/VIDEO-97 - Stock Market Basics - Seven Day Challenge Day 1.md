## The eligibility checklist

The speaker puts a series of questions to his student, and treats them as a filter anyone should apply to themselves before opening a trading account.

- Do you have a job, occupation or business producing income?
- Can you save something from it every month? If not, aggressive investing is not for you and you should stop here.
- Are those savings already going into mutual funds or a SIP?
- Do you and your whole family have health insurance — not just you?
- If you are the earning member of the household, do you have term insurance?
- Do you own your home?
- How close are you to retirement? Stock market investing is aggressive investing, and age matters.

Only the money left after income, insurance and children's education — surplus money — belongs in the market.

## Why a stock market exists

- Large businesses need capital to expand beyond what their trading profits fund. The market is the channel through which the public can put money into them.
- Primary market means buying before listing, where your money goes directly to the company. The video uses the National Stock Exchange, still unlisted, as the example.
- Secondary market is after listing, where shares change hands between investors and you can exit whenever you choose.
- A private business cannot legally raise money from the public: a partnership caps at 50 people, beyond that it must become a private limited company, and past 200 it must list on an exchange. The Sahara case is cited as what happens when money is taken outside this structure.
- The protection the exchange gives is counterparty risk management — the buyer and seller never meet and never need to trust each other. CDSL and NSDL handle the clearing side in India.
- What the exchange does not protect you from is price. A share can fall to almost nothing on the company's own performance; that is business risk, not fraud, and the balance sheet is open for you to examine.

## Process over returns

- Investing is long term; trading is short term. Both are a process you keep repeating, not a series of individual bets.
- No single trade guarantees a profit or a loss. The speaker's instruction is to stop thinking about returns altogether in aggressive investing.
- The ladder he describes: a fixed deposit pays x, so you move to mutual funds for a little more, and to the market for a little more than that. Wanting a little more is reasonable; expecting a thousand times more is not.
- Risk ladder as stated: fixed and recurring deposits and post office schemes at the low end, conservative and arbitrage funds in the middle, direct trading and investing at the aggressive end.

## Two kinds of risk, and diversification

- Unsystematic risk is risk you can manage or avoid. Systematic risk comes from the system itself and cannot be avoided by anyone.
- The student is asked whether the Uttarakhand cloudburst and the Punjab floods were unsystematic, answers yes, and is told this is wrong — natural disasters and events like Covid are systematic, and in those every portfolio falls together.
- The unsystematic case given is a person holding their entire life savings in gold near a record price. A 15–20% fall would hurt them badly, and the fix is a spread across gold, fixed deposits, conservative funds, aggressive funds and equities.
- The same logic applies to cash: money split across four bank accounts survives a withdrawal restriction on one of them, while everything in a single bank gets frozen with it.
- The homely version offered is money kept in several pockets while travelling rather than one.

## What the video tells you to do

- Clear the checklist above — savings, family health cover, term cover — before putting money into the market.
- Invest only surplus money, and keep it spread across several instruments instead of one.
- Split bank balances across multiple accounts rather than holding everything in one bank.
- Trade a defined process with discipline and stop forecasting your own returns.

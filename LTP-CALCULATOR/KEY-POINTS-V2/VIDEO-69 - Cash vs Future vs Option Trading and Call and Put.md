# Cash vs Future vs Option Trading and Call and Put

## In one line

Day three of the beginner challenge: what actually differs between cash, futures and options, why the speaker says a new trader should start in cash rather than options, and how to use volume near the price line to pick what to trade.

## What you can and cannot buy

- Indices — Nifty, Bank Nifty, Fin Nifty and Midcap Nifty — have no tradable spot. You watch the spot number but you trade the future or the option.
- Individual stocks can be traded in spot as well as futures and options, because a stock is a single company while an index is a basket of them.
- Futures and options trade only in lots. Nifty's lot is 75, Bank Nifty's is 35, and every stock has its own — the examples used include 250 and 475.
- In spot you can buy a single share; in futures you must take the whole lot or a multiple of it.
- Money required differs sharply. A stock future at around 2,685 with a lot of 250 is about ₹6.7 lakh of contract value, but margin brings the cash needed down to roughly ₹1.5 lakh.

## Ownership versus a rental agreement

- A share bought in spot is yours. You can hold it for a year or fifty years, and no margin is involved.
- A short sale in spot must be bought back the same day — intraday only.
- A futures position is compared to renting a shop: it runs until expiry, then you either close it or roll it into the next contract, and rolling costs money.
- That is why futures come with margin and spot does not: you are renting exposure, not buying the asset.

## The ladder the speaker recommends

- Ranked by capital needed: spot is the most expensive, futures less, options the least.
- Ranked by risk, the order inverts: spot carries the least risk, futures more, options the most.
- His reasoning by analogy: someone with very little money takes the largest gambles, and a wealthy person will not risk what he has.
- His recommended path is spot intraday first, then options or futures once you are making money, then back into spot for investing. He says most people do the opposite because brokers advertise and influencers push options as the cheap way in.
- He also states plainly that intraday trading should only be done with genuinely surplus money.

## Choosing direction — the drills

- If you expect the price to fall: sell the future, or buy a put. If you expect it to rise: buy the future, or buy a call.
- Simple practice questions are run through with the student on live stocks — "this will be at X in twenty minutes, buy or sell?" — until the answer is automatic.
- Profit in futures comes from the sell price being higher than the buy price, in either order. Which came first does not matter.

## Why prices move, and why volume decides the instrument

- LTP means last traded price, and is the same as the current market price. Price changes only when a buyer's bid and a seller's offer match, so every tick is one completed trade.
- A price that stays frozen for long stretches means very few people are trading it.
- The **imaginary line** is the marker between the two strike prices the spot price currently sits between. The speaker states this line and its name are his own invention, absent from Indian option chains until he added it, and now copied widely.
- Method taught: look at the volume on both sides immediately around the imaginary line, and compare instruments on that basis.
- Illiquidity is priced with an example: a stock last traded at 977 with only one buyer bidding 950 forces a sale 27 below the market, multiplied by the lot size. A crowded market would have produced competing bids at 960, 965, 970.
- On the day shown, Nifty carried far more volume than Bank Nifty, which carried more than Midcap Nifty, with Fin Nifty lowest. Among the stocks compared, HDFC Bank beat Ashok Leyland, which beat OFSS.

## What the video tells you to do

- Trade the future or the option of an index; do not look for an index spot to buy.
- Before entering, check the lot size and the margin so you know the real money at risk.
- Close a spot short the same day; plan a futures position around its expiry and the cost of rolling.
- Compare volumes around the imaginary line before choosing an instrument, and prefer the higher-volume one.
- When two instruments give the same setup at the same time, take the trade in whichever has the better volume.

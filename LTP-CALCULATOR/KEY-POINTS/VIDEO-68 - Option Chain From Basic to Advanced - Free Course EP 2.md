## Why most people buy options rather than futures

- The Nifty lot size is 75 shares. You must buy 75, or a multiple of 75 — 150, 225, 300 — never a single share. The comparison used is buying bananas by the dozen.
- At 25,600 a full lot is worth about ₹19.2 lakh, and the margin needed is roughly ₹1.5 lakh.
- Anyone without that capital buys a **portion** of the move instead, which is what an option premium is.

## The insurance analogy

- Insuring a ₹1 lakh bike yourself would mean keeping ₹1 lakh in the bank permanently. Instead you pay a ₹3,000 premium and the insurer reserves the ₹1 lakh for you.
- If the bike is stolen, the insurer pays. If nothing happens for a year, the ₹3,000 is gone. With 1,000 customers the insurer collects ₹30 lakh and only pays out for the few accidents.
- **Every strike price is an insurance company.** Each one offers to sell you its premium instead of making you buy the whole futures contract.
- The difference from real insurance: here the premium can come back to you. Index options expire every Thursday rather than after a year.
- The call side promises to return however many rupees the market closes **above** that strike at expiry. The put side returns however many rupees it closes **below** it.

## The replayed expiry that proves it

The session replayed the 3 July expiry, with spot near 25,495 in the morning.

- The 25,500 call premium was about ₹45. Its break-even is therefore 25,545 — every rupee above 25,500 is returned, so ₹45 of return needs a 25,545 close.
- If the market had closed at 25,550 you receive ₹50, a ₹5 profit. At 25,600 you receive ₹100. At 25,700 you receive ₹200, a ₹155 profit on a ₹45 cost.
- The market actually closed at 25,405. Every call strike at or above that expired worthless — 25,500 and 25,450 both went to zero, since a premium never goes negative.
- Below the close, the premium equalled the exact gap: 25,400 call was ₹5, 25,350 was ₹55, 25,300 was ₹115, 25,250 was ₹155.
- The put side mirrored it: 25,450 put was about ₹45, 25,500 was ₹95, 25,550 was ₹145, while 25,400 and 25,350 puts were worthless because the close was above them.
- Conclusion: a rising market raises call premiums and sinks put premiums, and a falling market does the reverse. So buy a call if you expect a rise and a put if you expect a fall.

## What the video tells you to do

- Before buying a premium, work out its break-even: the strike plus the premium on the call side, the strike minus the premium on the put side.
- Remember an option cannot go below zero, so a call above the closing price and a put below it are both worth nothing at expiry.
- Decide direction first, then pick the side. Do not pick a strike because its premium looks cheap.
- Use the tool's historical replay to step through a past expiry and check the arithmetic for yourself.

## What volume actually is

- Volume is created by a completed trade: a buyer and a seller matched. Buy five SBI shares and someone sold five, so five units of volume appear.
- Every executed trade sets a new Last Traded Price. Volume and LTP are therefore locked together — volume cannot change without the price changing.
- Volume resets to zero each session. It runs from 9:15 am to 3:30 pm and starts fresh the next day.
- The video is about volume on the option chain, per strike price, not the volume bars at the bottom of a price chart.

## How volume sits across the strikes

- Volume appears at every strike, not only at the one the viewer happens to be watching. The speaker calls the assumption that all traders sit on one strike a common myth.
- In-the-money strikes carry light volume; out-of-the-money strikes carry heavy volume. The two sides mirror each other — heavy Put activity in OTM puts sits opposite thin Call activity at the same strike.
- Example given from the chain: at 24600, roughly 83,877 trades on the Put side against 578 on the Call side; at 24650, about 44,970 on the Put side against 208 on the Call side.
- OTM strikes carry the writing activity. Institutional money — FIIs, DIIs, mutual funds — writes options through algorithms rather than buying Calls and Puts all day. Their target is a 16-25% annual return through a repeated process, not an overnight profit.
- For intraday trading the speaker says only the heaviest-traded strikes matter, because the deep-OTM expiry-focused machines are indifferent to intraday movement.

## The second-highest volume rule

- In the LTP Calculator the largest volume on each side is set to 100% and highlighted in blue; every other strike is shown as a percentage of it. The second-highest is highlighted in yellow.
- The speaker's central claim: the highest volume is what holds the market at a strike. The second-highest volume is what guides the market's next direction.
- The question to ask is whether the 100% volume is moving toward the second-highest strike. If yes, the market moves in that direction; if no, it does not.
- The chain labels the movement: "shifted from top to bottom" means the volume moved from a higher strike to a lower one, which creates bearish pressure. "WT" stands for weak towards top, which points bullish.
- The percentage attached to that guidance matters as much as its direction. A weak-towards-top reading falling from 96% to 94% to 93% means the bullish guidance is fading while the downward reading strengthens.
- Worked example from that day's replay: at 9:15 the 100% volume sat at 25200 with the market near 25119. The guidance flipped downward toward 25100, the bearish percentage climbed step by step (78%, 84%, 88%, 93%, 97%, 99%), and the index fell roughly 50 points alongside it.
- Once the second-highest reaches 100% it becomes the highest, the yellow highlight shifts elsewhere, and the reading restarts.
- Downside is capped at the strike holding the market. To fall further, the Put side's second-highest volume must also start guiding down. In the example it did not — it stayed flat and neither bold nor highlighted — and the fall stopped there.

## What the video tells you to do

- Read the option chain by strike, not the chart's volume bars, when judging direction.
- Find the highest volume on each side, then find the second-highest and watch whether the gap between them is closing.
- Track the direction label (shifted top-to-bottom, or weak towards top) together with its percentage. A rising bearish percentage means pressure is building.
- Treat the strike carrying the highest volume as the limit of the move until the opposite side's second-highest volume also turns.

Part one of strike price selection: what intrinsic value and time value actually are, how they split every premium, and why an at-the-money option can lose money even when the market returns to your entry price.

## Where a premium comes from

- The farmer-and-trader analogy: a farmer fears no rain, a buyer expects a good crop, so they fix a price today for delivery on a set date. That agreed price is a futures price, and the money each side accepts for the waiting period is time value.
- **On expiry day** a premium is pure intrinsic value. Call premium equals spot minus strike; put premium equals strike minus spot.
- Demonstrated on Nifty expiring at 19,545 on 4 October: the 19,500 call closed near ₹45, 19,450 near ₹95, 19,400 near ₹145. On the put side, 19,550 was about ₹5, 19,600 about ₹55, 19,650 about ₹105.
- Every strike on the wrong side of the close expires at zero.
- **Before expiry** premium = intrinsic value + time value. The 19,500 call showed intrinsic value 45.7 plus time value 81.1, which adds to the traded premium of about ₹126.9.
- The **IVTV** tab in the LTP Calculator splits every strike into those two figures.

## In the money, out of the money, at the money

- A strike has intrinsic value only on one side. Those strikes are in the money — on the app's display, the ones above the imaginary line on the call side and below it on the put side.
- Strikes with zero intrinsic value are out of the money. They consist purely of time value. The speaker's blunt description: you are paying for hope, like sponsoring an unknown actor in the expectation that he becomes a star.
- **At the money is defined here as the strike carrying the highest time value**, not simply the strike nearest spot. It is always one of the two strikes around the imaginary line, and the same strike carries the highest time value on both the call and the put side.
- At the money is the strike with the most power to deceive, because its time value is at its peak and has the furthest to fall.

## Why a break-even move still loses money

- Time value at the money starts decaying as soon as the market moves away from that strike.
- So a call bought at the money, taken into profit and not booked, can come back to the exact spot price it was bought at and still show a loss. The spot is unchanged; the time value is not.
- Most Indian option buyers trade at the money, which is precisely where this decay is largest.

## The link to reversals

- A writer earns most where time value peaks. The speaker's argument is that the price at which time value reaches its peak is where the writer commits most heavily — and that is the reversal price the tool calculates.

## What the video tells you to do

- Open the IVTV tab and read every premium as its two parts before choosing a strike.
- Identify the at-the-money strike by the highest time value, not by proximity to spot.
- Treat out-of-the-money strikes as pure time value and price the risk accordingly.
- Book profits rather than assuming a return to your entry price protects you.

## Where support and resistance actually come from

- Support and resistance are just the market's current boundaries, and they are set by the traders in it — nobody external draws them.
- They are demand and supply. High demand against low supply lifts price, the reverse drops it, and equal amounts leave price flat.
- On the option chain, volume and open interest are where that demand shows up. Open interest is what writers are asking for; volume is what intraday traders are actually executing. A level only becomes real when the execution agrees with the writing.
- The pen analogy: you can offer to sell a pen for ₹1 lakh, but the pen is not worth ₹1 lakh until somebody buys it at that price.
- In the live example, the highest call volume sat at 25,900 and the highest put volume at 25,800, while the highest open interest was far away at 26,000 and 25,500. The speaker trades the volume levels, because that is where intraday participants are committing.

## Reading the highlighted percentages

- The tool highlights a strike once the shift pressure reaches about 75%. Below that the number is shown but not highlighted.
- The direction of the highlight tells you which way a level is trying to move. When both the call side and the put side pushed towards higher strikes, the scenario label changed from slight bullish to bull run.
- When only one side was shifting up, the market stayed merely bullish. When the call-side percentage started falling while the put side rose, the label changed to both side risky.
- The practical read: if resistance at 25,900 is shifting up and support at 25,800 is also shifting up, a short here loses. If the resistance percentage is falling while you are long, that is the exit signal.
- The speaker's claim is that watching which side the highlights move is enough — you do not need to have learned support and resistance separately.

## Why every call buyer is building resistance

- A call writer and a call buyer are complementary, not opposed; the writer's level does not exist until a buyer pays for it.
- So buying calls at a level adds another brick to the resistance there, regardless of what the buyer wants to happen.
- The speaker's conclusion: calls should be bought near the bottom of the range, where resistance is far above, and puts should be bought near the top. Buying calls after the market has already run up simply strengthens the level it will reverse from.
- In the example the buy level was S Risky at 25,760, which is the reversal price for the 25,850 strike where about 4 lakh of volume had built up.

## The two ways to trade

- **With the direction of the market** — the default, and what the scenario label supports.
- **Against the direction** — allowed only when the level you are fading is not trying to extend. If resistance percentages are still climbing, a put bought there will lose even though the level looks like the top.
- Whichever side you take, the exit is defined in advance: leave the moment the percentage moves against your position.

## Options as insurance, not as a cheap entry

- The common advice — cash if you have little money, futures if you have more, options if you have least — is reversed here. Options are called the instrument for people with the most money, because the risk is largest.
- The car insurance analogy: a ₹6 lakh car carries a ₹10,000 premium. Buying an option without an underlying position is like buying car insurance when you own no car.
- The stated purpose of options is to protect an existing cash or futures position. Example: short SBI futures at 913 with a fear of a rise to 923. Buying the 915 call for ₹5 means that if SBI expires at 925 the call is worth ₹10 against a ₹12 futures loss, leaving a net loss of about ₹3 instead of ₹12.

## Intrinsic value and why cheap premiums are dangerous

- On expiry day only intrinsic value survives; time value goes to zero. A strike above the closing price on the call side is worth nothing.
- Demonstrated on a Nifty expiry that closed near 25,850 with an adjusted price of 25,843: the 25,800 call was left at about ₹43, the 25,750 at ₹93, the 25,700 at ₹143, each 50-point step adding 50 to the premium. Every strike above the close was zero.
- The hedging example uses Cholamandalam Finance bought near 1,760. Buying the 1,760 put for about ₹13 caps the worst case at ₹13, because the put gains rupee for rupee as the stock falls.
- Buying the cheaper 1,740 put for ₹5 instead does not cap the loss until the stock has already fallen 20 points, so the worst case becomes about ₹25 — nearly double, for a premium that looked a third of the price.
- The rule stated: the cheaper the premium you buy, the larger the risk you are actually carrying.
- "Naked buying" is buying premium with no underlying position and no idea of the intrinsic value you are betting against. The video treats this as the main way retail option buyers lose.

## What the video tells you to do

- Read the volume columns, not just open interest, to find the levels intraday traders are defending.
- Treat 75% as the threshold at which a shifting level starts to matter.
- Before entering, check whether the level you are trading against is extending. If it is, stay out.
- Set the exit condition before entry — a specific percentage level at which you leave, regardless of profit or loss.
- Buy calls near support and puts near resistance, not after a move has happened.
- When hedging, evaluate the worst case rather than the profit case, and prefer a premium near the money over a cheap far one.
- Do not buy naked options unless you can compute the intrinsic value at expiry.

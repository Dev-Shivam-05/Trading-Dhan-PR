Day three of the beginner series: the practical differences between cash, futures and options, why an index can only be traded through derivatives, and how to use volume to decide which instrument is worth trading at all.

## Index versus stock

- There are four NSE indices in regular use: Nifty, Bank Nifty, Fin Nifty and Midcap Nifty. An index is a team assembled from individual stocks — all the banking names together become Bank Nifty, mid-sized companies become the Midcap index.
- **You cannot buy the spot value of an index.** The number shown as spot is only a reference; trades happen in the futures or the options.
- An individual stock can be traded in all three: spot, futures and options.
- Mutual fund schemes are built on the same idea. A "Nifty Midcap 100 Index Fund" holds the top 100 midcap names, a "Largecap 150" holds the largest 150, and so on.

## Lot sizes and the money each instrument needs

- Futures and options trade only in lots. Quoted in the session: Nifty 75, Bank Nifty 35, Fin Nifty 65, Midcap Nifty 140, Angel One 250, Bharti Airtel 475.
- Angel One at about ₹2,685 with a 250 lot means a contract worth roughly ₹6.71 lakh, but broker margin brings the requirement down to around ₹1.5 lakh.
- In spot there is no lot. You can buy a single share at its market price, and no margin is given.
- Options cost least of all. Buying a Nifty put premium at ₹90 costs ₹90 × 75 = ₹6,750, against roughly ₹1.5 lakh of margin for the equivalent futures position.

## What you actually own

- **Spot buy** — the shares are yours. You can hold them for a year, ten years or a lifetime. This is the owned shop.
- **Spot sell** — you must buy back the same day. A short in cash cannot be carried overnight.
- **Futures** — a rented shop. The agreement ends on expiry, and you either close the position or pay to roll it into the next month's contract. Rolling costs money.
- The speaker's ordering by capital: spot needs the most, futures less, options least. Risk runs the opposite way — the option buyer carries the most risk.
- His analogy: the person with the least money takes the biggest risks, riding three to a bike without helmets, while the person with more takes a car with a seat belt. A trader with ₹150 crore has no reason to trade intraday.

## The advice on where to start

- The speaker's stated rule is to begin with **intraday trading in cash**, build capital slowly, move to options, then futures, and eventually put profits back into cash investments.
- Most new traders do the opposite and start in options, because broker advertising and YouTube influencers push that route.
- If your capital is only a few thousand rupees, his suggestion is a ₹100 or ₹200 stock where you can buy 20 or 50 shares, rather than an index option.
- He notes that when 500 people repeat the same thing, the one person disagreeing is treated as the fool — and applies that to the crowd's preference for F&O.

## How a price is actually formed

- Buyers and sellers place bids at many different prices. A trade happens only where a buyer and a seller match.
- The number on screen is the **LTP — last traded price** — not a live current price. It is the same thing normally called CMP. This is where the tool's name comes from.
- If the price sits unchanged for long stretches, few people are trading and the instrument is bad for you.
- The reason given: with ₹8.8 lakh to transact, you want the crowded market. In an empty one, if the last trade was 977 and the only buyer will pay 950, you are forced to accept 950 — a ₹27 loss per share multiplied by the lot size. In a busy market competing buyers bid the price back up.

## Using volume to choose what to trade

- The **imaginary line** in the middle of the option chain marks where the market price sits, between the two nearest strikes. Volume should be compared around that line on both sides.
- Comparisons made live: Ashok Leyland showed more volume than OFSS; HDFC Bank more than both.
- Among indices, Nifty showed about 11.59 lakh against Bank Nifty's roughly 31,000 and Midcap Nifty's 40,000–45,000, with Fin Nifty the lowest. The trading preference is therefore Nifty, then Bank Nifty, then Midcap Nifty, then Fin Nifty.
- The practical rule: if the same setup appears in two instruments, take the one with higher volume.

## What the video tells you to do

- Decide the direction first. Expecting a rise means buy futures, or buy a call. Expecting a fall means sell futures, or buy a put.
- Two practice questions used: if HDFC Life will be at 767 in an hour, buy now and sell there; if M&M Finance will be at 257 in twenty minutes, sell now and buy back there.
- Check the imaginary line and the volume on both sides before committing to an instrument.
- Avoid anything where the last traded price barely moves.
- Understand why the price moves rather than following signals mechanically.

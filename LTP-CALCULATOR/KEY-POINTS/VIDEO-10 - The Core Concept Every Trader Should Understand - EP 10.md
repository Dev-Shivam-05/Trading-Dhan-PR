## In one line

The first actual trade setup in the series: when both support and resistance are strong, the market stays between them, and you enter at their extensions rather than at the levels themselves.

## Scenario one of the Chart of Accuracy

- The Chart of Accuracy is the speaker's framework for deciding whether a level will actually reverse the market. This episode covers only its first scenario: strong support and strong resistance at the same time.
- Analogy used: a polythene bag filled with water and sealed hard on both sides. If both seals hold, the water cannot escape from either end. Strong support and strong resistance behave the same way, and the market simply travels between them.
- In that condition the market reverses at both ends repeatedly, and those reversals are where the money is.

## How the trades are taken

- Buy at the extension of support, book profit at the extension of resistance, and the reverse on the other side.
- A level is only good for so many touches. The first touch is the safest trade; the second is riskier; after that the level starts reading weak and the market can break out one side or both.
- If a level extends past you, do not square off in panic — wait, and average in once, then exit when it comes back.
- One or two trades a day at most. Repeatedly buying call then put then call all day only produces losses.
- Values shift by about five to seven points, never much more, because the data is a one-minute snapshot live and a two-minute snapshot in historical mode.

## What the video tells you to do

- Wait until both support and resistance read strong before trading this setup.
- Skip the first signal of the morning — he calls it untradeable because it comes too early.
- Take no new trade after 2:30 PM.
- Mark all four values on the chart before trading: the two extensions and the two diversions one strike beyond them.
- Go through historical data on Nifty, Bank Nifty and stocks, find days where both levels were strong, and check how far the market actually travelled. He presents this as emotional training as much as analysis — the usual mistakes are exiting a position out of fear as it approaches the target, or panicking when a level is touched repeatedly.

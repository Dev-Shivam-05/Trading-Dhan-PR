## In one line

A guest talk by Ritesh Shukla of Kotak Mutual Fund making the case that India's economy has structurally changed, and that money you can leave alone for five years belongs in mutual funds rather than in trading.

## The case that India has changed

- Living standards, by his framing: before Covid, about 0.5% of Indians lived a "French" standard of life and about 15% an Indonesian middle-class one. Today roughly 3% are in the first group and about 26% in the second. Around 70% still contribute little, and only about 30% of the population drives the economy today.
- Inflation stayed in the 3.5% to 6% band while world inflation ran near 9-10%.
- Foreign exchange reserves are now around $620 billion, against 1991 when India pledged gold in London to raise money.
- Between 2013 and 2024: power production and capacity roughly doubled, electrified railway roughly tripled, highway length roughly doubled, ports went from about 1,400 to 2,800 units of capacity, and metro lines grew eight to nine times from a small base.
- India now receives orders to build locomotives for Germany, a country it used to import them from.
- Public sector bank NPAs fell from the 9-11% range to about 1% or below. His point: no economy grows without a healthy banking system, because nobody funds a business entirely from their own capital.
- In 2008 India's GDP was roughly 30% of Brazil and Russia combined. Today it roughly equals their combined total.
- Six of the world's ten fastest-growing cities are Indian: Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata.
- State-level comparison: Maharashtra's GDP today matches all of India's in 2005; Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand together match India's in 2001; Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Gujarat each roughly match India's in 2000.
- Startups number around 90,000 to 95,000, with funding up roughly eight to nine times from an earlier average of $40 billion.
- Amul, a cooperative, has a turnover larger than the named multinational FMCG firms and targets $220 billion by 2047.

## Digital payments and leakage

- India's data consumption exceeds that of the US and China combined. Digital transactions run at roughly 50 billion a day.
- The bigger effect is direct benefit transfer. His fertiliser example: rather than paying a ₹200 per bag subsidy to the manufacturer against an invoice, the government pays the farmer directly.
- His claim: around $100 billion a year was leaking from the economy in 2015 and roughly $300 billion by today's date is no longer lost. When transfers went direct, the intermediaries collecting that money turned out not to exist.
- Same pattern in education subsidies. Once fees were paid to students rather than colleges, the "ghost" colleges with no real students disappeared.
- Anecdote used to show how deep UPI runs: a London-based friend could not pay cash for a metro ticket or a cigarette in Bangalore, while in London only credit cards work.

## Manufacturing and the China shift

- India is now the world's second largest mobile phone manufacturer, exporting roughly $22-23 billion worth. Apple began manufacturing in India in 2018.
- Toys shifted from imported to exported.
- After Covid, roughly 135 companies looked to move out of China. Only around 13 to 15 came to India. His comparison: in Vietnam, one single company contributes half the country's tax GDP.
- His request to the audience: buy Indian-made goods where a local option exists, because a roughly $500 billion trade bill sends earnings back out.

## The investing argument

- The last 18 months were difficult: money rotated to China after October 2024, earnings disappointed through early 2025, then tariff announcements, Operation Sindoor and the Israel conflict followed.
- Recommended horizon is five years. In any five-year window he expects one bull phase, one bear phase and one flat phase.
- His expected-return formula: **FD rate + inflation + risk premium**. Worked example: 7.5% FD rate plus 4.5% inflation plus a risk premium of 1% for large cap, 2% for mid cap, 3% for small cap — giving roughly 12-13%, or 14-15% in a good market.
- Covid illustration: whether you invested at Nifty 26,000, 28,000 or 30,000 during the crash, all three are profitable at around 73,000-74,000. Only not investing loses.
- On timing: invest today, withdraw when you need the money. Booking profit creates a reinvestment problem, because that money then has to be placed somewhere again.
- Core and satellite: keep trading capital and long-term capital in separate buckets, and never move money between them.
- Savings target: invest 40% of income. You earn between roughly 20 and 60 years of age, and must fund the years after 60 out of that window.
- Compounding example: ₹1,000 a month for 30 years at 14% grows to about ₹85 lakh.
- Retail SIP flows now roughly match and offset foreign institutional selling.

## Fund manager questions from the audience

- **Does a large fund struggle to sell?** No. SEBI does not mandate a cash level; holding cash is the fund manager's call. Indian market turnover is large enough that ₹2-4 lakh crore of liquid stock can be sold without difficulty. Fund size has no bearing on performance. Liquidity only becomes a real constraint in small and micro cap.
- **Why do AMCs stop accepting money into small cap funds?** When the team judges that prices have run well past intrinsic value and buying looks unnatural, they close subscriptions rather than deploy capital badly. His example: a stock they value at ₹100 trading at ₹140-150 with no supporting fundamentals — they would also look to exit it. The same could in principle be done in large cap.
- **Mutual funds do not guarantee returns.** His reply: nothing is guaranteed, but not investing carries a guaranteed loss, because inflation erodes the money regardless.

## What the video tells you to do

- Keep money you will not need for five years in mutual funds and money for trading in a separate account. Do not move capital between the two.
- Estimate expected returns as FD rate plus inflation plus a risk premium of 1%, 2% or 3% depending on cap size.
- Invest roughly 40% of income.
- Do not check the NAV daily. Treat it the way you treat land you have bought.
- Start now rather than waiting for a level.

## Where the day's move can end

- In scenario one, where both support and resistance are simply strong, the day's travel is bounded by the extension of resistance and the extension of resistance +1 above, and the extension of support and the extension of support -1 below.
- Extension +1 is the reversal price of the next strike out. It is the final boundary. If price passes it without reversing, close the position for the day and accept that something outside the framework is driving the market.
- The first level on each side is the risky line, the second is the moderate line. In the worked example these came out as R Moderate 1,329 above, with S Risky 1,291 and S Moderate 1,274 below.
- Stop loss sits at extension +2, the max pain line - 1,357 on the upside and 1,242 on the downside in that example. None of these are round numbers; each is the reversal price of a specific strike.
- These are the levels the app draws automatically, but the video's point is that they only make sense once you know which strike each one came from.

## Single factor and double factor levels

- A level built on volume alone, or on open interest alone, is single factor. A level where both sit at the same strike is double factor.
- For a single-factor level the reading is simple: whatever that one factor is doing, the level is doing. If the volume is weak towards bottom, the resistance is weak towards bottom.
- For a double-factor level the rules are deliberately asymmetric, depending on which direction the shift moves relative to the money.
- Resistance turns weak towards bottom if either factor moves that way, but it only turns weak towards top when both factors do.
- Support turns weak towards top if either factor moves that way, but only turns weak towards bottom when both do.
- Practice cases run through CG Power, Nifty, Bank Nifty, Bank of Baroda, Hindustan Zinc, Britannia, Asian Paints, DMart and others, identifying the factor type and the resulting pressure in each.

## Predicting the next scenario

- The method: look at each side's current pressure, ask what single change would flip it, and read off which of the nine scenarios that would produce.
- From a bull run, one side turning bearish moves you to scenario eight or nine (risky on both sides); one side merely turning strong moves you to scenario five.
- From scenario eight, the bullish side turning bearish gives a blood bath. From a blood bath, either side turning bullish gives eight or nine.
- In a blood bath, one side moving further bearish changes nothing - only a turn to bullish changes the scenario.
- The weighing-scale image: if the percentage at the lower strike is growing, the lower strike is getting heavier and the market is bearish. If the higher strike is getting heavier, bullish.

## Where the screen lags the market

- The displayed scenario only updates when a percentage crosses 75%. A level falling from 95% towards 80% has already turned bullish in substance while the screen still prints blood bath.
- Similarly, a level that remains weak towards bottom but moves its weak point up from 4,000 to 4,100 has turned bullish in pressure while keeping its label.
- The speaker's instruction is to read this manually and understand that the flagged blood bath will not arrive.

## What the video tells you to do

- Exit the position when the scenario changes against it. Do not hold on because it might still come back. It may come back four or six times out of ten; the time it does not, you are trapped.
- Before entering, decide what change would end that scenario, so you recognise it while it is happening rather than afterwards.
- Always cross-check the support and resistance label printed at the top of the screen against the strike you picked from the percentage column. The guest is corrected on this repeatedly.
- Watch the percentage move through 65, 68, 70, 72 rather than waiting for it to reach 75 and surprise you.

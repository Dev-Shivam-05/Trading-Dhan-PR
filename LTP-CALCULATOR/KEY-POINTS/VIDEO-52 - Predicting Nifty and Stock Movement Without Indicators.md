## The 9:20 lines and what they mean

- The tool draws four horizontal lines after analysing the market between 9:15 and 9:21. Before 9:21 only the "live" lines exist; the 9:20 lines appear once that window closes.
- Top to bottom the four lines are moderate resistance, risky resistance, risky support, moderate support.
- The upper two are the put-buying zone; the lower two are the call-buying zone.
- "Risky" and "moderate" refer to stop-loss size, not to the quality of the signal: a risky line carries a wide stop, a moderate line a narrow one.
- A history player lets you replay any past date tick by tick at 1x, 2x or 4x speed, which is how the whole session is demonstrated.

## The rules the speaker attaches to those lines

- Take no trade, call or put, unless price is actually sitting on one of the four lines. Anything in the middle of the range is to be ignored — this is presented as the cure for overtrading.
- Each line is good for one trade per day. If price returns to the same line a second time, that second touch is not traded.
- The target is the reversal price directly opposite on the option chain. In the example a put was entered at a level of 598 on the put side, and the matching value on the call side, around 547-549, became the target. Price bottomed near 545, so the target was reached.
- A risky put has no stop loss. Instead you average: add the same number of lots you already hold. If you cannot fund that second lot, do not take the risky line at all — take the moderate line, where a normal stop applies.
- The extension of a reversal price is 10 points beyond the outer reversal price; the stop loss sits 10 points inside the nearest one.
- The tool has a calculator field: type the index level into the reversal price box and it returns the matching option premium for entry and for the stop loss.

## Applying it to five random stocks

- Stock charts show the same four lines, labelled C1, C2, P1, P2. Once C1 is traded, P1 is not taken that day; once C2 is traded, P2 is not taken.
- A "bullish risk" and "bearish risk" number sits above the chart. Zero is the best condition for that side, one is tolerable, two or more means skip the trade. Dalmia showed bullish risk of five, so no bullish trade; Hind Copper showed three, so the call side was dropped.
- If a stock opens exactly at its C2 line, no trade is taken in that stock all day (the Chambal Fertilisers example).
- If a line's stop loss or target is already reached inside the first or second candle, that trade is skipped. Balkrishna's first candle high was 2698 against a stop around 2600-something, so the trade was void before it started.
- Results shown: BPCL gave one stop-out on C1 and one C2 trade that never reached target and was closed at cost; the Gujarat Gas example hit target on both P2 and C2.

## What the video tells you to do

- Wait for price to touch a line before doing anything; do not trade the space in between.
- Check the bullish/bearish risk number before taking the side you were planning to take.
- Read the target off the opposite side of the option chain rather than setting your own.
- If you take a risky line, be ready to double the position instead of using a stop loss; otherwise trade the moderate line.
- Save the rules image posted on the speaker's handle and keep it beside you while trading.
- Pick five stocks and follow them for a full week rather than scanning everything.

## Worth knowing

- These are the speaker's own rules attached to his own tool, not an established method. Several of the prices in the transcript are mangled by speech recognition.
- Advising an average-down instead of a stop loss on the "risky" line is the riskiest instruction in the video; it converts a defined loss into an open one.

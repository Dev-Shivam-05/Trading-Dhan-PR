A podcast with chartered accountant Sumeet Mongia covering why India dropped American-style options, how a small-cap pump and dump is actually structured, how large money behaves differently from retail money, and one legitimate way to reduce tax on trading gains.

## Why American options disappeared from India

- Indian stock options were once American style, marked CA and PA rather than CE and PE.
- Under a European option you can only exit if a buyer appears on the exchange. The guest's example: a call bought at ₹8 is worth ₹22 to buyers while sellers quote ₹40, and you are stuck with the spread.
- An American option let the holder request the exchange to close the position. The exchange then had to pick one of the short sellers at random and force the assignment on them, which the guest calls an "unlucky draw".
- That forced-assignment process needed heavy monitoring and left assigned writers aggrieved, so India moved to European options.
- Liquidity has since removed the original problem: options no longer trade far below intrinsic value, so the exchange facility is not needed.

## How big money behaves

- Mutual funds receive continuous inflows and must deploy them. Unlike retail, they add more as the price rises, because the money keeps arriving.
- Their effort goes into selecting the company; after that the job is to hold. The guest's retail takeaway: if a mutual fund starts buying a stock, more fund money is likely to follow it.
- Mutual funds take limited exposure per company, so a single collapse cannot wreck the NAV. The guest notes no Indian mutual fund has defaulted. The industry is quoted at about ₹87 lakh crore.
- Mutual funds are largely barred from futures and options. The newer SIF/AIF structures are allowed to trade them.
- Scale changes ambition: someone arriving with ₹20,000 wants it to become several lakh; someone managing ₹2,000 crore asks whether he can get 3%.
- Both speakers say a trader needs about a decade of watching the market — or at minimum 200 hours with it — before the same event reads as opportunity instead of disaster.

## Anatomy of a pump and dump

Presented deliberately as devil's advocate.

- Incorporate a small services company (placement, IT, websites) for ₹1–2 crore in a single room.
- Take it public through an SME IPO at a nominal ₹5 crore valuation, allotting shares to friends and associates rather than genuinely distributing them to the public.
- Have those holders trade between themselves — a buyer bid at ₹20 against a seller at ₹20 on a share opened at ₹10 doubles the company's valuation on a hundred shares traded.
- Repeat over the two to three years the company must remain on the SME platform, then build real volume in order to sell the holding to the public.
- SEBI counters this through ASM and GSM surveillance. The broker must show an alert at the buy button when the PE ratio is out of line with profits, or when price and volume swings are extreme. The alert does not block the purchase — the choice stays with the buyer.
- Rajesh Exports, RCom, Idea and Yes Bank are cited as cases where retail holders carried the loss. Punishment deters future fraud but cannot reconstruct who lost what across the chain of transactions.

## The tax point

- Before 31 March, book the loss on a position that is under water, then buy it back the next day. The cost is effectively restored while the loss enters the books.
- That loss can be carried forward for eight years and set against future gains.
- The guest presents this as a fully legitimate method, while joking that the finance minister may close it in a future budget.

## What the video tells you to do

- Test a new strategy or algorithm in a small account, never in the main trading account. The guest's own worst mistake was exactly this: the team removed the hedge on an algo-generated call buy in excitement, the market reversed, and one trade cost a whole month.
- Book March losses on positions you intend to keep, and re-enter, to bank the carry-forward.
- If you are under 25, spend three months working inside a broking or research firm before trading your own money.
- Deal with SEBI-registered advisers, and treat lifestyle displays and promised multiples as the warning sign.
- Ask yourself whether you want to earn quickly or earn more. The guest says the market is not for the first answer.

## In one line

Why a cash-market position taken without a matching option premium is an uninsured position, worked through an ITC example that shows how a cheap put caps the loss.

## What LTP actually means

- LTP is the **last traded price** — the price at which the most recent trade between a buyer and a seller was executed. That is why the tool is named the LTP Calculator.
- Each new print means a new trade happened at that price. The market price is simply the last completed transaction, nothing more.
- Analogy used: when you ask a land rate in your area, the answer is the price a buyer paid three months ago. The next deal may be higher or lower.
- LTP can be read on spot, futures and options, and the speaker's position is that reading it correctly answers most questions about the market.

## The insurance analogy

- A ₹6 lakh car insured for a ₹25,000 annual premium: the insurer can promise full replacement because it collects that premium from many people at once.
- With 10,000 policies at ₹25,000, the insurer takes ₹25 crore. If 500 of those cars are written off, it pays out ₹6 crore and keeps the rest.
- The option writer is in the insurer's position — selling premium to many buyers, taking losses on a few and profit on the rest. The buyer is the single policyholder.
- A stock position with no option against it is described as a **naked trade**. No insurance company will cover a share price, so the options market is where that cover is bought.

## The ITC worked example

- Setup: ITC trading at ₹430, lot size 1,600, so 3,200 shares is two lots. A ₹10 move is worth ₹32,000.
- Insurance: buy the 425 put at about ₹2.50. That costs roughly ₹8,000 for 3,200 shares.
- If ITC rises ₹10, the cash position gains ₹32,000, the put expires worthless, and the net is about ₹24,000.
- If ITC falls, the put gains value as it moves in the money, so the cash loss is offset rather than open-ended.
- If ITC expires at ₹400, the cash position is down ₹30 per share but the 425 put is worth roughly ₹25, so the net loss is small and known in advance instead of ₹96,000.
- Paying a higher premium — buying a closer strike, or the 430 put at a higher price — reduces the profit on an upmove but reduces the loss on a downmove by the same logic. The trade-off is direct.
- A put bought further out of the money costs less and leaves more upside, but covers less of the fall.

## The behaviour the insurance is meant to fix

- Without cover, a position that first moves ₹1, ₹2 or ₹3 against the entry produces fear. The speaker's claim is that 99 out of 100 traders exit there, and then miss the recovery.
- With a put in place, the same adverse move is tolerable because the loss is capped, so the position can be held to the intended horizon.
- If the market reverses back to the entry, the choice is to close the put and keep the cash position, or to roll the put down to a lower strike (for example from 425 to 400) if the fall is expected to continue.

## What the video tells you to do

- Treat any cash or futures position without a matching option as uninsured, and size the fear accordingly.
- Buy a put against a long position. Choose the strike by how much loss you are willing to carry, since a nearer strike costs more and protects more.
- Decide in advance whether you will book the put's profit and keep the underlying, or roll the put down as price falls.
- Accept that the premium is a permanent cost on winning trades. That is the price of the cover.
